In January 2023, I walked into my first day as a 23-year-old software engineering intern. Back then, I was so overwhelmed that my heart would race just giving a two-sentence update during daily stand-ups.

Today, at 26, I’m an SDE 2—still in the same company, still on the exact same team, leading customer meetings and mentoring new engineers.

Looking back, the progression looks linear on paper. In reality, it took making mistakes in public, hitting burnout, rebuilding my health and confidence outside of work, and slowly realizing I could trust my own judgment.

### The 2023 Internship: Building Without a Safety Net

My six-month internship (January to June 2023) threw me straight into the deep end. My project involved building AWS services for our team's use case, migrating data from a data lake to Caspian, and generating an OpenSearch dashboard link to demo to internal users.

Today, engineers have mature AI assistants to help debug stack traces or sanity-check configurations. Back in early 2023, ChatGPT had just entered the market, and AI wasn't woven into our daily workflows. Every unfamiliar AWS configuration, data migration snag, and OpenSearch query meant digging through documentation, reading internal wikis, and learning through trial and error.

I made plenty of mistakes along the way. Misconfigured permissions, broken pipelines, dashboards that wouldn't load—every error felt massive when I didn't yet have the experience to tell a minor hiccup apart from a real crisis. Generating a single working dashboard link felt like climbing a mountain.

Without years of intuition to lean on, I compensated with sheer effort: I took obsessive notes, documented every step of my work, and poured everything I had into getting that demo right. When June arrived and I received my full-time return offer, it proved that relentless effort could bridge the gap.

### The Post-Internship Trap: Fear of Mistakes and Decision Paralysis

Joining full-time didn't magically erase the overwhelm—it just changed its shape.

Over the next couple of years, I worked closely with almost every member of my team across different projects. While I learned a lot from watching senior engineers debug and weigh trade-offs, I also carried a constant fear of making mistakes. In an internship, errors feel expected; as a full-time engineer, I felt like getting something wrong would expose me as unqualified.

That fear made me dependent on my teammates for decisions—even the simple ones. *Should I structure this API this way? Is this edge case worth handling now? What if I break something?*

Despite having context on our codebase and systems, I was filled with self-doubt. Whenever a call landed on my plate, my instinct was to double-check with someone more experienced just to feel sure I wasn't missing something obvious.

### December 2025: Burnout, Noise, and Learning to Say No

By December 2025, pushing hard toward promotion while buried in self-doubt caught up with me. I hit a wall of pure exhaustion and burnout.

That phase taught me a hard truth about external feedback: not everyone pointing out your flaws is trying to help you grow. Some people will genuinely support you and show you *how* to uplift yourself; others will only list what you're missing without offering a path forward. Trying to please everyone and prove myself to every critic was draining me dry.

I realized I could never keep everyone happy all the time. I started setting boundaries, saying "no" when my plate was full, and focusing on actionable growth rather than endless self-criticism. Around that time, I also received an award recognizing my work. Regardless of the politics or intentions behind awards in big organizations, I knew deep down that I had earned it through sheer grit.

### The Turning Point: When the Team Shifted, I Stepped Up

The biggest catalyst for my growth at work happened when my team changed. Five of my seniors got promoted and moved on to their own paths, leaving just one senior engineer and me alongside a wave of new joiners.

Suddenly, there was nobody left to hide behind—and that vacuum gave me the exact scope I needed to grow.

- **From asking for direction to unblocking others:** Having started mentoring even before the seniors left, I stepped fully into guiding new teammates, reviewing their code, making architectural calls, and unblocking their day-to-day work.
- **Owning end-to-end delivery:** I learned to write full design documents from scratch, drive the implementation, and break down clear instructions for newer engineers to execute alongside me.
- **Proving it through people:** I trained two interns who both converted into strong full-time hires—and I had the concrete data points to back up their impact.

Managing others while holding deep context on our systems unlocked a completely new level of energy and confidence. The person who used to ask everyone what to do had become the person teammates came to for answers.

### Rebuilding Beyond the Screen

The confidence I found in engineering didn't happen in isolation—it grew alongside the changes I made in my personal life.

A year ago, I joined the gym and committed to taking care of my physical health. The transformation went far beyond strength: my hormones balanced out, irregular periods disappeared, and chronic back pain faded away. Feeling strong in my body gave me a quiet, steady confidence in my mind.

Around the same time, I started sharing my journey on my Instagram page. At first, stepping in front of the camera felt intimidating, but today I show up without anxiety or the fear of being judged. Talking openly about my experiences—and inspiring others who are hesitating to start their own pages—reminded me how good it feels to step outside my comfort zone.

### Where I Stand at 26

Today, I can look at a problem from multiple angles, propose practical solutions, guide junior engineers with confidence, and lead meetings directly with customers. The days of my voice shaking during a routine stand-up feel like a lifetime ago.

**Confidence doesn't happen overnight—you have to overcome the fear of self-doubt first, and consistently gaining real knowledge is what builds that foundation.** Self-doubt doesn't vanish the day your SDE 2 promotion goes through, and there will always be new challenges that test me. The difference now is that I have the courage to step out of my comfort zone and take ownership anyway. Looking ahead, I see a confident woman who knows what she is capable of—and I know this is only the beginning of how far I can rise.
